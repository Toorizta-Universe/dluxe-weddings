# Where in Costa Rica should you get married? A regional guide.

By **Madelyn Sánchez** — May 12, 2026 — 16 min read — Planning Guides

---

## The short answer, in one paragraph

Costa Rica has six regions that I would actually marry a couple in, and they are not interchangeable. **Tamarindo, Las Catalinas, and Playa Flamingo** are the driest and sunniest, the easiest logistically, and the best fit for couples who want a guaranteed-sun beach-and-headland wedding. **Peninsula Papagayo** is five-star resort territory — Four Seasons, Andaz — and the right answer for couples who do not want to think about logistics at all. **Nosara** is the yoga-jungle alternative, more intimate, more bohemian, smaller guest counts, a slower rhythm. **Santa Teresa** is the fashion-and-surf coast, design-forward, the youngest and most of-the-moment scene. **Manuel Antonio** is rainforest theatre — monkeys in the canopy, jungle-and-ocean dramatics, real rain. And **Uvita and the Osa Peninsula** is the wildest, the most remote, the right answer when you want true escape and a guest list small enough to mean it.

## A note before we begin

I have produced weddings in every one of these regions. I have stood on the headland at Papagayo in 30-knot January wind holding a bouquet for a bride, I have run a generator at a Nosara villa when the power went out an hour before the ceremony, and I have shot a ceremony in Manuel Antonio under a tent at 3pm in May because I had read the radar correctly that morning. What follows is not the tourism-board version. It is what I would tell you across a table.

The regions of Costa Rica are not "more or less the same with different beaches." They feel, weather, and read differently. The right region for your wedding is the one whose temperament matches yours. Below — honestly, region by region.

## <a id="tamarindo"></a>Tamarindo, Las Catalinas, and Playa Flamingo

*The driest coast, the easiest logistics, the gold-and-blue wedding most couples picture when they say "Costa Rica."*

**Where it is.** The North Pacific coast of Guanacaste province, roughly an hour south of the Liberia (LIR) international airport. Tamarindo is the largest town; Las Catalinas is a planned coastal village twenty minutes north; Playa Flamingo and Playa Conchal are in between. Most luxury weddings in this corridor happen at private villas on the headlands above the beaches, not in the towns themselves.

**Airport and drive.** LIR is the only airport that matters here. Liberia is a small, easy international terminal — most of your guests will be through customs and in a shuttle within an hour of landing. From LIR to Las Catalinas or Playa Flamingo is about 45 minutes on good highway. To Tamarindo, an hour. There is no other region in Costa Rica with this kind of guest-logistics simplicity.

**Weather and season.** This is the driest region in the country. The dry season (December through April) is reliable enough that I will tell a couple, with reasonable confidence, that it will not rain on their March Saturday. The wet season here is mild — Guanacaste gets a fraction of the rainfall that the southern Pacific or Caribbean coast receive, and the rains arrive as afternoon showers, not as days of weather. November weddings can be extraordinary. October is the one month I generally steer couples away from on this coast.

**Vibe and aesthetic.** Golden tones. Pacific blue. Dry tropical forest — gnarled, sun-bleached, beautiful. Open ocean horizon. The light here is harder and warmer than anywhere else in Costa Rica, which is a wedding photographer's dream and a 1pm-portrait-session problem. Ceremonies happen at 4:30 or 5pm for a reason. The landscape is not jungle; it is more like *Baja with better swimming*. It reads less Costa Rica-cliché and more *editorial coast.*

**Signature venues.** [The Point Luxury Villa](https://www.thepointluxuryvilla.com) above Playa Langosta, with its long pool deck above the surf. [Vista Hermosa Estate](https://www.vistahermosaestate.com), the headland buyout above Tamarindo. [Casa Chameleon Las Catalinas](https://www.casachameleonhotels.com), the boutique hotel-of-record for the Las Catalinas village. Plus a half-dozen private villas in Playa Flamingo and Conchal that work beautifully for buyouts of 60–100 guests.

**Right for the couple who wants:** guaranteed sunshine; the easiest possible guest experience; the option to marry on a headland with a 180-degree Pacific horizon; a wedding that photographs in golden light against scrub-green and ocean blue; a town within driving distance for non-wedding-day dinners and excursions.

**Common misunderstanding.** Couples assume Tamarindo-the-town is where they will be marrying. They have read about Tamarindo as a backpacker surf town and it makes them nervous. In practice, no luxury wedding I have produced takes place *in* Tamarindo town. The town is restaurants and surf shops and after-dinner drinks for guests; the wedding is on a headland twenty minutes away, in a private villa most couples have never seen on any postcard.

**Honest pro.** The most consistent weather in Costa Rica. If you cannot risk rain, this is your region.

**Honest con.** The dry-season look is golden and brown, not lush green. Couples who picture a "Costa Rica jungle wedding" sometimes arrive in February and feel the landscape is more dry than they expected. The first hour of the site visit usually resolves this — the headlands and the ocean do the work — but it is worth saying in advance.

## <a id="papagayo"></a>Peninsula Papagayo

*The five-star resort answer. Manicured. Logistics-free. The wedding that takes care of you while you take care of your people.*

**Where it is.** A 2,300-acre private peninsula on the northwestern tip of Guanacaste, twenty-five minutes north of LIR airport. It is the highest-end resort enclave in Costa Rica. The peninsula is gated, designed, and owned by a single development company; what is on it is two flagship resorts — the Four Seasons and the Andaz — plus a Prieta Beach Club, a marina, and a small portfolio of private residences.

**Airport and drive.** Thirty minutes from LIR. The closest region to the airport in this entire guide. Your guests can land at noon and be in their rooms with a welcome drink by 1:30.

**Weather and season.** Same dry-season pattern as the rest of Guanacaste — reliably sunny December through April, mild green-season showers May through November. December and January get wind on the headland, which is sometimes lovely and sometimes a problem for floral installations and candlelit ceremonies. I have built ceremonies around the wind here three times, and the wind has won twice.

**Vibe and aesthetic.** Manicured. The peninsula is landscaped to within an inch of its life — the dry forest has been cleared, replanted, and curated for sightlines. The architecture is high-end resort design: pale stone, dark wood, infinity pools edge-cut to ocean. The signature view is not *wild* coast; it is *composed* coast. This is the answer for couples who want luxury legibly — who want guests to step out of the car and immediately understand the level of the wedding.

**Signature venues.** The **Four Seasons Papagayo** with its three ceremony lawns and the famous ocean-side reception terrace. The **Andaz Papagayo** with its more architectural, design-forward, slightly younger feel. The **Prieta Beach Club** for the welcome dinner or rehearsal beach party. Private villa rentals on the peninsula (the Casa Cosmos category) for couples who want resort-of-record convenience without taking over a hotel.

**Right for the couple who wants:** hotel-of-record convenience; one address for guests, transport, accommodation, ceremony, and reception; a multi-day program where the resort handles half the logistics; the security of an international five-star brand standing behind every detail; a wedding that does not require explaining anything to anyone.

**Common misunderstanding.** Couples assume that "Four Seasons wedding" means "all-inclusive package." It does not. The Four Seasons is your venue and your hotel. Your planner, your florist, your photographer, your design, your bilingual officiant — these are still yours to bring in. The resort handles its lane beautifully; what makes the wedding yours is everything outside the resort's lane.

**Honest pro.** The most logistics-free wedding you can produce in Costa Rica. For couples with older guests, multi-generational guest lists, or guests who have never traveled internationally, this is genuinely the kindest option.

**Honest con.** It can read more *resort* than *Costa Rica.* If you have come this far because you want the country to be a character in your wedding — the rainforest, the wildness, the sense of arrival in a place — Papagayo can feel like a beautifully landscaped version of anywhere. That is the trade. For some couples it is exactly right. For others it is the wrong sentence in the right country.

## <a id="nosara"></a>Nosara

*The yoga-jungle answer. Smaller, slower, more intentional. The antithesis of a resort.*

**Where it is.** On the Nicoya Peninsula, an hour and a half south of LIR, on Costa Rica's central Pacific coast. The last thirty minutes of the drive is gravel road — this is the price of admission, and it matters. Guests cannot accidentally land in Nosara. They have to *commit* to Nosara.

**Airport and drive.** LIR is the airport. The drive is 90 minutes, of which the final stretch is unpaved. Some couples charter a small plane from LIR (Nature Air, Sansa) to the Nosara airstrip — 25 minutes in the air, no gravel road. For weddings of 30 or fewer where every guest matters, this is sometimes worth the cost.

**Weather and season.** Drier than Manuel Antonio but wetter than Tamarindo. Reliable dry weather December through April. The green season in Nosara is genuinely green — the jungle here is denser than Guanacaste's dry forest, and a May or November wedding here looks more *Costa Rica jungle* than a Tamarindo wedding would. The trade-off is that the gravel road becomes a serious factor in heavy rain.

**Vibe and aesthetic.** Jungle meets ocean. Surf town. Wellness-leaning. There is a long-standing yoga community here (Bodhi Tree, Blue Spirit), a strong American expat presence, and a quiet, slower energy than Guanacaste's headland coast. Nosara is barefoot. It is *bohemian without being a costume.* The aesthetic is unfinished wood, raw linen, jungle green, and ocean blue.

**Signature venues.** This is a buyout-villa region. There is no marquee resort. Boutique villas and small jungle hotels — The Gilded Iguana, The Hidden Garden, Lagarta Lodge, Olas Verdes, and a portfolio of private rental estates — host the great Nosara weddings. Most couples here buy out a single villa for the weekend and produce the wedding inside it.

**Right for the couple who wants:** the opposite of a resort wedding; a smaller, more intimate guest list (25–70 people); a "weekend in the jungle with our people" feel; a wedding that reflects an actual relationship with yoga, wellness, surfing, or slow living — not as a theme but as a real value; vendors and a setting that read *Costa Rica* without reading *tropical-paradise stock photo.*

**Common misunderstanding.** Couples assume Nosara is small enough that "we will just figure it out when we get there." Nosara is a small town with a serious vendor scarcity at the luxury tier. The few great florists, photographers, and caterers who work this region book a year ahead. You can absolutely produce a beautiful Nosara wedding — you cannot produce it on a casual six-month timeline.

**Honest pro.** The most "Costa Rica" feeling on this list. If your couple has spent time at a yoga retreat, or you imagine your wedding weekend including a morning surf and a sunset *temazcal*, Nosara is where you actually live that life — not where you stage it.

**Honest con.** The gravel road is real. Older or less mobile guests find the final leg of the drive uncomfortable. If half your guest list is over 70, this is the wrong region.

## <a id="santa-teresa"></a>Santa Teresa and Mal País

*The fashion-and-surf coast. Design-forward. The most of-the-moment scene in Costa Rica right now.*

**Where it is.** The southern tip of the Nicoya Peninsula, about as far from an airport as you can get while still being in Guanacaste. Two and a half hours by road from LIR, including a ferry crossing of the Gulf of Nicoya. Many couples — and most planners — fly clients in on a 25-minute charter from LIR to the Tambor or Tárcoles airstrip instead.

**Airport and drive.** LIR is the airport for international guests. From LIR, the road option includes a ferry and roughly two and a half hours of driving; the charter-flight option is 25 minutes in the air to Tambor, then 45 minutes by road. For weddings above 40 guests, plan a charter for at least the couple and the wedding party, and a coordinated ferry-and-road program for the rest. Logistics here are real.

**Weather and season.** Dry-season weather is excellent — December through April is reliable. Green season is wetter than Tamarindo, drier than Manuel Antonio. The wind on this coast in January and February is genuinely strong and shapes ceremony orientation.

**Vibe and aesthetic.** Minimalist-tropical. Architecturally ambitious. This is the Costa Rica that you have seen on Instagram in the last three years — the design hotels (Hotel Nantipa, Pranamar, Florblanca), the linen-and-concrete villas, the surf-club restaurants, the expat scene of fashion brands, photographers, and design studios that have moved here from New York and Berlin. Santa Teresa is *of the moment* in a way that no other Costa Rica region currently is. It also reads younger.

**Signature venues.** Hotel Nantipa, Pranamar Villas, Florblanca, Hotel Tropico Latino on the beach side. A growing portfolio of architectural private villas — Casa Marbella, Villa Manzu (technically Papagayo, but flown into here for design references) — for buyouts. There are fewer "iconic" wedding venues here than in Tamarindo or Papagayo. The destination is still developing its luxury-wedding infrastructure, which is part of why it feels so fresh.

**Right for the couple who wants:** a design-forward, fashion-conscious wedding; a setting that reads *2026* and not *2014*; a guest list of design-aware friends who will appreciate the references; a tighter guest count (30–80) where the aesthetic can be controlled; a surf morning the day after the wedding.

**Common misunderstanding.** Couples treat Santa Teresa as a mood and not as a logistics problem. The mood is correct. The logistics are real. A 90-guest wedding here is genuinely harder to produce than a 90-guest wedding in Tamarindo, and the cost reflects it — vendor travel from San José or Liberia adds 15–25% to several line items.

**Honest pro.** If your aesthetic instincts run toward Architectural Digest more than Brides Magazine, this is your region. The work that comes out of Santa Teresa weddings is the most contemporary in the country.

**Honest con.** The logistics tax. The drive is long, the ferry is real, the charter is expensive, and the vendor pool is shallower than in Guanacaste. You are paying for design-forward in cost and complexity.

## <a id="manuel-antonio"></a>Manuel Antonio and Quepos

*Rainforest theatre. The jungle-and-ocean Costa Rica most couples imagine when they first start dreaming about getting married here.*

**Where it is.** The Central Pacific coast, an hour and a half south of San José. Manuel Antonio is the headland community south of the town of Quepos, named for the small but world-famous national park that ends at one of Costa Rica's most photographed beaches. The luxury wedding venues sit on the ridge above the park, looking down through canopy at the Pacific.

**Airport and drive.** San José (SJO) is the closer airport — three hours by road. Liberia (LIR) is three and a half hours. For couples with U.S. guests, SJO is almost always the right answer; the flight options from American hubs are stronger and the connections are easier. For weddings of any size, schedule a coordinated shuttle program from SJO — the road is mostly highway but it is still three hours.

**Weather and season.** This is where Costa Rica's weather story changes. Manuel Antonio is much wetter than Guanacaste — the Central Pacific gets nearly twice the rainfall of Tamarindo, and the wet season is genuinely wet. The reliable dry months here are January, February, and March, with December and April acceptable. May through November is real rain, and I have produced beautiful weddings in those months but only with serious tent contingency and a couple who has accepted that the photographs will be jungle-after-rain, not golden-hour-on-headland.

**Vibe and aesthetic.** Lush. Dramatic. Cinematic. The canopy above Manuel Antonio is full of howler monkeys, sloths, and toucans — the wildlife shows up at your wedding whether you wanted it to or not, and it is the most charming uninvited guest list you will ever have. The light here is softer and more diffuse than Guanacaste. The aesthetic is jungle green, ocean below, mist in the morning, fireflies at dusk.

**Signature venues.** [Villa Punto de Vista](https://www.villapuntodevista.com), the headland estate buyout that has hosted some of the most-photographed weddings in Costa Rica. [Castle of Oz](https://www.castleofoz.com) at Playa Hermosa just south. **Rancho Pacifico** further south near Uvita. The boutique side of the Tulemar community for smaller weddings. The Hotel Mariposa, Si Como No, and Tulemar Bungalows for hotel-of-record options.

**Right for the couple who wants:** the rainforest as the visible backdrop; a wedding where wildlife is part of the experience, not adjacent to it; the lush, deep-green Costa Rica that magazines photograph; the dramatic ridge-and-canopy view rather than the open-horizon Pacific.

**Common misunderstanding.** Couples assume "Costa Rica is Costa Rica" — that if they had a good experience in Tamarindo in dry season, a wedding in Manuel Antonio in October will be similar. It is not. The rain pattern is genuinely different. October in Manuel Antonio is wet for real, not for picturesque-afternoon-shower reasons. If your heart is set on this region and your date is fixed in green season, we need to design around tented infrastructure from day one.

**Honest pro.** The most *jungle Costa Rica* wedding photography in the country. If you want your wedding album to look like it was shot inside a rainforest, this is where you marry.

**Honest con.** Rain risk is real outside of January–March. If you cannot tolerate weather uncertainty and your date is fixed, weight your decision toward Guanacaste.

## <a id="uvita"></a>Uvita and the Osa Peninsula

*The wildest, the most remote, the most ecologically immersive. The right answer when you want true escape and a guest list small enough to mean it.*

**Where it is.** The South Pacific coast, beginning at Uvita and the Whale's Tail beach formation and extending south into the Osa Peninsula and Drake Bay. This is the most ecologically intact region in Costa Rica and one of the most biodiverse zones on Earth. National Geographic has called the Osa "the most biologically intense place on the planet." That is not a wedding-marketing line; it is what scientists actually say about this peninsula.

**Airport and drive.** SJO (San José) is the practical airport. From SJO, Uvita is four hours by road on the Costanera Sur — a beautiful but long drive, particularly with multi-generational guests. The Osa Peninsula proper is closer to five hours, plus a boat in some cases. For weddings here, charter flights are not a luxury upgrade — they are part of the program. A 50-minute Nature Air or Sansa charter from SJO to Quepos, Drake, or Palmar Sur airstrip is how most couples move their guests, particularly anyone who would find the road exhausting.

**Weather and season.** This is the wettest region in this guide. Dry season runs January through March, with December and April acceptable. April through November is genuinely wet, with September and October near-impossible. If you marry in Uvita or the Osa, you marry in dry season or you marry under a serious tent.

**Vibe and aesthetic.** Wild. Primary rainforest meets ocean. There is no "scene" here — no Instagram restaurants, no design hotels, no fashion-tourism economy. The luxury that exists here is the luxury of *escape* — small, deeply considered eco-lodges and private estates built into the jungle. The Whales-Tail coast offers whale watching from July through October, which overlaps with the wet season; some couples build a wedding around this rhythm and it is extraordinary.

**Signature venues.** **Rancho Pacifico**, the headland villa above Uvita, the closest thing to a flagship venue in this region. **Hacienda Altagracia** further inland for couples who want the central highlands rather than the coast. A growing portfolio of private jungle estates on the Osa Peninsula proper, none of which are household names but several of which are extraordinary. **Lapa Rios** for guests' lodging if you are producing a wedding on the peninsula itself.

**Right for the couple who wants:** a deeply intentional, small wedding (18–50 guests); a setting that is unmistakably *wild* and not curated; a guest list that has self-selected on the basis of "we will travel far for this"; a couple's natural rhythm that includes hiking, conservation, marine biology, sustainability — not as a brand but as a value; the kind of wedding that operates as much as a four-day shared experience as it does as a ceremony day.

**Common misunderstanding.** Couples sometimes choose Uvita because the photography looks dramatic and then discover that the region is not designed for 100-guest production. The infrastructure simply is not there — vendor pools are smallest, road access is longest, weather risk is highest. A 30-guest Uvita wedding can be among the most beautiful weddings I produce in a year. A 110-guest Uvita wedding is a logistics challenge that is rarely worth the cost or stress.

**Honest pro.** The most extraordinary natural setting in Costa Rica. The wedding photography here is on a different axis from anywhere else in the country.

**Honest con.** Distance. Weather risk. Vendor scarcity. Cost of the charter-flight program. The Osa rewards the couple who wants this, and punishes the couple who only wanted the photographs.

## <a id="how-to-decide"></a>How to decide — five questions

If you have made it this far and you are still between regions, these are the questions I would walk you through if we were on the phone.

**1. How important is "guaranteed dry weather" to you, on a 1–10?**
If it is a 9 or 10, you are in Guanacaste — Tamarindo, Papagayo, or possibly Nosara. If it is a 5 or below and you want the *most* Costa Rica visually, Manuel Antonio or Uvita open up.

**2. How much do you want your guests to feel "logistics-free"?**
If the answer is "very" — for example, you have older guests, kids, or guests who do not travel internationally — Papagayo wins. If your guest list is well-traveled and they will read the journey as part of the experience, Nosara, Santa Teresa, and Uvita become viable.

**3. How many guests are you planning to host?**
- 18–40: Nosara, Santa Teresa, or Uvita.
- 40–70: Any of the six.
- 70–110: Tamarindo, Papagayo, Manuel Antonio.
- 110+: Tamarindo or Papagayo, almost always.

**4. What is the visual you keep returning to?**
- Open ocean horizon, golden, scrub-and-blue → Tamarindo / Las Catalinas.
- Manicured infinity-pool resort with ocean below → Papagayo.
- Jungle-meets-beach, raw and unfinished → Nosara.
- Architectural villa, minimalist, design-forward → Santa Teresa.
- Lush canopy, monkeys in the trees, ridge above the park → Manuel Antonio.
- Wild, primary rainforest, whales offshore → Uvita / Osa.

**5. What is your couple's actual rhythm?**
This is the most overlooked question and the most important. Do you wake up early to surf? Do you go to yoga together? Do you take photographs on hikes? Do you read about ecology? Do you spend Saturdays at restaurants? The region that already matches your shared rhythm will produce a wedding that feels like you, not a wedding that asks you to play a role for a weekend.

## <a id="faq"></a>Frequently asked questions

### Which region of Costa Rica is best for a wedding?

There is no single best region. For *most* North American couples planning a luxury wedding, **Tamarindo / Las Catalinas / Playa Flamingo** is the most consistently right answer because of weather reliability and ease of guest logistics. **Papagayo** is the right answer for couples who want a resort-of-record. **Nosara** and **Santa Teresa** are right for design-forward couples with smaller guest lists. **Manuel Antonio** is right when you want the rainforest to be visible. **Uvita and the Osa** are right for small, ecologically intentional weddings. The wrong region is the one you choose on the basis of a single photograph without understanding the trade-offs.

### Should I fly into San José or Liberia for my wedding?

If your wedding is in Guanacaste — Tamarindo, Papagayo, Nosara, Santa Teresa — fly into Liberia (LIR). If your wedding is in Manuel Antonio, Uvita, or the Osa, fly into San José (SJO). For Santa Teresa specifically, LIR is closer geographically but involves a ferry; many couples charter a short flight from LIR or SJO to the Tambor airstrip instead.

### Is Tamarindo or Papagayo better for a wedding?

Different products. **Tamarindo** (and its corridor — Las Catalinas, Playa Flamingo) is the right answer for couples who want the open-headland, ocean-horizon, private-villa wedding. **Papagayo** is the right answer for couples who want the manicured five-star-resort wedding where the venue handles half the logistics. Same weather, different temperament. I have couples who arrive at the planning table sure they want Papagayo and end up at The Point in Tamarindo, and the reverse. The site visit is what settles it.

### What is the difference between Nosara and Santa Teresa for a wedding?

Both are smaller, more bohemian-leaning peninsula regions. **Nosara** is yoga-and-jungle, wellness-leaning, older as a destination, with a stronger expat-American community and an unfinished-wood aesthetic. **Santa Teresa** is design-and-surf, fashion-leaning, younger as a destination, with a Brooklyn-and-Berlin expat layer and a more minimalist-architectural aesthetic. Nosara feels like *retreat*. Santa Teresa feels like *editorial*. Choose for which sentence describes you.

### Where in Costa Rica is the driest in May?

**Guanacaste** — Tamarindo, Las Catalinas, Playa Flamingo, Papagayo, and to a lesser degree Nosara. May is the first month of green season everywhere in Costa Rica, but Guanacaste's rains are the latest to arrive and the lightest when they do. May ceremonies in Tamarindo are routinely beautiful. May ceremonies in Manuel Antonio or Uvita are a real bet.

### Is Manuel Antonio too rainy for a wedding?

Not in January, February, or March — those months are reliably dry and the region is extraordinary. Outside of those three months, rain risk rises significantly, and from May through November you should plan around the rain, not against it. A real tent contingency at $6,000–$15,000 is non-negotiable for green-season Manuel Antonio weddings.

### What is the most luxurious region of Costa Rica for a wedding?

**Peninsula Papagayo** is the most resort-luxurious — five-star branded properties, manicured infrastructure, logistics-free production. **Tamarindo and Las Catalinas** is the most private-villa-luxurious — The Point, Vista Hermosa Estate, Casa Chameleon Las Catalinas, and a portfolio of villa buyouts that operate at the highest design level in the country. Different definitions of luxury; both correct.

### Can I get married on the beach in Costa Rica?

Yes, in any of these regions. Public-beach ceremonies require a small municipal permit ($150–$400), and your planner handles it. Most luxury couples marry on a private headland, terrace, or villa lawn overlooking the beach rather than directly on the sand — sand ceremonies are romantic but logistically harder for guest seating and floral installation.

### Where in Costa Rica do celebrities get married?

The two regions that quietly host the highest-profile weddings in Costa Rica are **Peninsula Papagayo** (for the privacy of the resort security model and the convenience of the airport) and **Manuel Antonio** at **Villa Punto de Vista** (for the visual drama and the privacy of the headland estate). The Osa Peninsula is the third — for couples who want true privacy and are willing to pay for the charter-flight logistics.

### What is the best region of Costa Rica for a small wedding under 30 guests?

**Nosara**, **Uvita**, and **Santa Teresa** are all built for this scale. A 25-guest wedding at a Nosara villa or an Osa jungle estate can be the most beautiful version of a destination wedding — fully buyout, private, intentional. At 30 guests in Papagayo or Tamarindo you can still have an extraordinary wedding, but you may be choosing the smaller properties of a region built for larger production.

### What is the best region of Costa Rica for a 100-guest wedding?

**Tamarindo / Las Catalinas / Playa Flamingo**, **Papagayo**, and **Manuel Antonio** are the three regions that produce 100-guest weddings comfortably. Vendor depth is strongest, venue capacity matches, and the road and airport logistics work for the size. Nosara, Santa Teresa, and Uvita are technically possible at this scale but rarely the right answer.

### Are there year-round wedding regions in Costa Rica?

Practically, no — but **Guanacaste** comes closest. Tamarindo, Las Catalinas, Playa Flamingo, and Papagayo can produce beautiful weddings in 11 of 12 months. October is the one month I would steer couples away from across all of Costa Rica, regardless of region. The Caribbean coast has a reversed weather pattern (driest September–October) but I do not generally produce luxury weddings on that coast — the infrastructure does not match the wedding-design tier.

### Is the Caribbean coast of Costa Rica good for weddings?

Honestly, no — not at the luxury-wedding tier this guide is built around. Puerto Viejo and the Caribbean side have a different climate, different culture (Afro-Caribbean rather than Pacific Tica), and a different infrastructure. The luxury-wedding ecosystem in Costa Rica lives on the Pacific. There are exceptions — small symbolic ceremonies, very intentional couples with a Caribbean connection — but as a general rule, the six regions in this guide are the six I would marry a couple in.

### Which region is best for a green-season Costa Rica wedding?

**Tamarindo / Las Catalinas / Playa Flamingo** in May, June, or November. **Papagayo** in the same months. The green-season look in Guanacaste is golden-to-emerald, lightly humid, with afternoon showers that clear before sunset. The same months in Manuel Antonio or Uvita carry real weather risk.

### How far in advance should I pick a region?

Pick the region first — ideally six to twelve months before you start contacting venues. The region drives everything downstream: the airport, the design language, the vendor pool, the season, the guest experience. I have seen couples spend three months on venue tours before realizing they were touring in the wrong region; the venue had been a downstream symptom of the regional decision they had not made yet.

Costa Rica's six wedding regions feel and weather and read differently enough that getting the region wrong is a much bigger error than getting any single venue wrong inside the right region.

The right region for you is the one whose temperament *matches* yours. The dry, golden, open-ocean confidence of Tamarindo. The manicured, resort-anchored ease of Papagayo. The slow, bohemian intimacy of Nosara. The design-forward Santa Teresa surf scene. The rainforest theatre of Manuel Antonio. The wild, primary, ecologically intense Osa. Six versions of the same country. You only need one of them to be yours.
