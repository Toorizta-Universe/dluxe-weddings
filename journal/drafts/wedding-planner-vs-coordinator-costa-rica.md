### The short answer

> A **full-service wedding planner** is hired 12 to 24 months out and owns design, vendor sourcing, budget, contracts, logistics, and day-of execution; she typically costs 10–12% of total budget or a flat fee of $15,000–$30,000+ at the luxury tier. A **partial or month-of planner** steps in 2 to 4 months out, after the couple has booked venue and major vendors, and runs the final logistics and the wedding day for roughly $4,000–$8,000. A **day-of coordinator** is hired 4 to 8 weeks out, takes the plan you have built yourself, and executes the wedding day only for $1,500–$3,500. For a Costa Rica destination wedding, a day-of coordinator is almost never the right answer — the geography, the language, the vendor relationships, and the on-the-ground problem-solving require someone embedded in the country for the full planning cycle.

---

### The conversation I keep having

I have this conversation, in some form, almost every week.

A couple writes in. They have been to a friend's wedding back home in Austin or Toronto or San Francisco, where the friend "just hired a day-of coordinator and it was great." They have already chosen a venue in Costa Rica — usually a villa they fell in love with on Instagram. They have a Pinterest board. They have a budget. They are asking whether I can do day-of, because they think most of the planning is already handled.

Sometimes the answer is yes. Most of the time the answer is no, gently. And the difference between those two answers is what this post is about.

I want to be clear about something before we go further: a day-of coordinator is not a worse choice than a full-service planner. It is a *different* choice for a different kind of wedding. The mistake is not in choosing day-of. The mistake is in choosing day-of for a wedding that needs more than that — and not realizing it until month seven, when you are on a Zoom call at 11pm trying to confirm a florist's WhatsApp message in Spanish that you do not fully understand.

So. Let me lay the three roles out honestly. Then I will tell you which one fits which kind of wedding. Then we can talk about Costa Rica specifically, because Costa Rica changes the math.

### Role 1 — The full-service wedding planner

This is the work I do.

A full-service planner is hired 12 to 24 months before the wedding date and is involved from the first decision forward. We start before the venue is booked, before the date is locked, sometimes before the guest list is finalized. We are part of every conversation that shapes the wedding, not just the conversations that execute it.

**What we do.** Vendor sourcing and curation — we have working relationships with the photographers, florists, caterers, musicians, hair-and-makeup teams, officiants, transport operators, and rental houses we trust, and we know who is right for *your* wedding versus a different couple's. Design direction — color, style, environment, paper, florals, lighting, music. Budget — we build it, defend it, and tell you the truth about what is and is not realistic. Contracts — we read them, negotiate them, and flag the lines that will become problems if left as written. Timeline — for the planning months and for the wedding day itself, down to the fifteen-minute increment. Communications — every email, every WhatsApp message, every clarification with every vendor. And day-of — the execution, the troubleshooting, the quiet protection of the couple from any of it.

**What we don't do.** We do not officiate (unless we also happen to be officiants — I am, but that is a separate credential and a separate line item). We do not photograph. We do not cater. We are not the venue. We are the person who chooses the venue with you, books the photographer for you, signs the catering contract with you, and ensures all of those vendors execute on the wedding day as designed.

**What it costs.** At the luxury tier in Costa Rica, full-service planning typically runs 10–12% of total budget or a flat fee, depending on how the studio structures its fees. For a $75,000 wedding, that is $7,500–$9,000. For a $150,000 wedding, $15,000–$18,000. For a $300,000 statement wedding, $25,000–$40,000+. The fee structure varies by studio — some charge a flat fee that does not scale with the budget, some take a percentage, some do a hybrid. None of those are wrong. What matters is that you understand what you are paying for and what is included.

**When you should hire a full-service planner.** If you are planning a destination wedding. If your guest count is above 40. If you are building anything more than the wedding day itself (welcome dinner, brunch, Day-2 excursions — and you should be, for a destination wedding). If your budget is $75,000 or more. If you do not live in the country where you are marrying. If you do not speak the language fluently. If you have a demanding job. If you have any of the above in combination, you are squarely in this role's territory.

**Signs you actually need a different role.** You have already booked your venue, your photographer, your caterer, your florist, your officiant, your music, your hair and makeup, your transport, and your rentals. You have a complete timeline. Your budget is built and tracked. You are confident with vendor communications in the local language. You only need someone to run the wedding day. In that case, you do not need full-service. You need a coordinator. Honest answer: this is rare, but it happens.

### Role 2 — The partial or month-of planner

This is the middle path, and it is a real one — not a compromise.

A partial planner is engaged two to four months out. The couple has already done most of the heavy lifting: they have booked the venue, the photographer, the caterer, the florist, often the music. They have a budget. They have a guest list. What they don't have is the bandwidth, the time, or the experience to manage the last sixty days of logistics and run the wedding day itself.

**What we do at this tier.** We inherit the work already done, audit it, and flag anything that worries us. We take over vendor communications for the final stretch — confirmations, payment schedules, day-of logistics, walkthroughs, rain plans. We build the production timeline and the floor plan. We run the rehearsal. We execute the wedding day with our own team. If a problem appears, we own it.

**What we don't do at this tier.** We do not re-design what has already been chosen. We do not re-source vendors unless something has gone genuinely wrong. We do not rebuild a budget from scratch. We do not own the months before we were hired — which means if a contract was signed unfavorably or a vendor was booked who isn't right for the wedding, we work with what is in front of us. That is the deal.

**What it costs.** $4,000–$8,000 in Costa Rica at the luxury tier. Lower at smaller scales. Higher if the wedding is genuinely complex or multi-day even with partial scope.

**When you should hire partial.** You have planned weddings before. You have time. You have a venue and your major vendors locked in. You have the temperament for vendor management but not for the wedding day itself. You want professional execution without giving up creative control. This is a real, valid path — and for the right kind of couple, it works beautifully.

**Signs you actually need a different role.** You haven't booked your venue yet. You don't have a florist or caterer. Your budget is undefined. You are planning a multi-day event with welcome dinner, wedding, and brunch. You are planning at a private villa that requires its own infrastructure build. You are doing any of this from another country. Any one of those, and partial is going to feel like a coordinator showing up to fight a fire she didn't start. You need full-service.

### Role 3 — The day-of coordinator

This is where I want to be most careful, because I do not want to talk anyone out of a choice that genuinely fits them.

A day-of coordinator is hired four to eight weeks before the wedding. By that point, the couple has done absolutely everything: every vendor booked, every contract signed, every detail decided, every timeline drafted. The coordinator's job is to take that complete plan, walk into the wedding day, and execute it — vendor arrivals, ceremony cues, reception transitions, troubleshooting, line-of-sight on the bride so she never has to ask where her bouquet is.

**What we do.** Two or three pre-wedding planning calls. A walkthrough of the venue. A review of every vendor contract and timeline. The rehearsal. The wedding day itself, usually 10 to 14 hours on the ground. Sometimes the night-before vendor confirmations.

**What we don't do.** Anything before the four-to-eight-week mark. We do not source vendors. We do not design. We do not negotiate. We do not own the budget. We are not present for the months of planning. We are stepping in at the very end to execute someone else's plan.

**What it costs.** $1,500–$3,500 in Costa Rica. This is a real number for a real service — the lower end of which is roughly what a wedding photographer charges for two hours of engagement photos.

**When a day-of coordinator is actually the right choice.** A backyard wedding. A small local wedding under 40 guests. A wedding where the couple lives in the same city as the venue. A second wedding or vow renewal where everyone involved is local. A wedding in a venue the couple has been to many times before, with vendors they have personally interviewed and walked through the day with. A simple wedding, in other words. Day-of coordinators do beautiful work for the weddings that fit this shape.

**When a day-of coordinator is the wrong choice.** A destination wedding. A wedding in a country where the couple does not live. A wedding where the couple does not speak the local language fluently. A multi-day event. A private villa buyout requiring infrastructure (generator, kitchen build, custom rentals). A wedding above 50 guests. A wedding above $50K in budget. A wedding where any one critical vendor is being booked sight unseen.

I want to be honest here. I have inherited weddings as a last-minute coordinator. I have walked into Friday afternoon rehearsals and discovered the florist was confirmed for the wrong date, the photographer's flight had been cancelled, the venue's "in-house coordinator" was three weeks into a new job, and the couple was on the verge of tears in the welcome dinner. I have fixed those weddings. The couples have been kind about the outcome. But none of those weddings looked like the wedding the couple had been imagining for two years. We salvaged. We did not produce.

### A Costa Rica reality check

Now the Costa Rica part — which is most of why I wrote this.

A Costa Rica destination wedding is not the same kind of project as a wedding in your hometown. The differences are structural, and they all push in the same direction: toward full-service, away from day-of.

**1. Vendor communications happen in Spanish — usually.** The top wedding florists, the kitchen leads at private villas, the transport companies, the rental houses, most of the band-and-DJ teams. Their work is exceptional. Their working language is Spanish. Bilingual studios bridge that gap as part of the work; couples managing on their own often discover at month four that "yes we can do that" in a WhatsApp message and "yes we can do that for an additional 35% and only on Thursday" are two different conversations.

**2. The vendor relationships are personal, not transactional.** The reason a vendor agrees to a last-minute change at 4pm on a Saturday is usually that they have worked with the planner thirty times. They do not move that fast for someone they have never met. Costa Rica's wedding industry runs on long-standing relationships. A planner who is *in* those relationships is a different working asset than a coordinator parachuting in for the weekend.

**3. The geography is harder than it looks on a map.** Tamarindo to Manuel Antonio is six hours by road. San José to Papagayo is four and a half. A florist based in the capital working a Pacific-coast wedding means transport, lodging, per diem — and a planner thinking about whether all of that is worth it or whether a local team is the smarter choice. Without that kind of judgment in the loop, couples end up paying full San José rates plus full travel costs for a vendor a local-based team could have rivaled.

**4. The infrastructure is uneven.** Private villas often need generators, kitchen builds, additional rental infrastructure. National parks require permits. Beach ceremonies require municipal permissions. Some venues do not have reliable power for a full band-and-lighting load. None of this is on a "preferred vendor list." You learn it by producing weddings here, year after year. A coordinator hired four weeks out will not know any of it.

**5. The legal-and-symbolic distinction matters.** Most of my couples do the legal portion of their marriage in their home country and a symbolic bilingual ceremony in Costa Rica. The wrong information about this — sourced from a five-year-old blog post — can cost months of paperwork and several thousand dollars in apostille fees. A planner gets this right on the first call.

**6. The weather is regional, not national.** May in Guanacaste is mostly dry. May in Manuel Antonio is wet. October in Tamarindo can be lovely. October in the Osa Peninsula is genuinely raining. A coordinator does not have the regional weather literacy to design a venue choice around it. A planner does.

This is the part of the post that is going to sound like a sales pitch and isn't meant to. I would tell you the same thing if you were planning your wedding with someone else. *A Costa Rica destination wedding deserves a planner, not just a coordinator.* The work is too embedded in the place to be done in the last four weeks by someone who does not live in it.

### The most common mistake — the "venue coordinator" confusion

This is the question I should have led with, because it is the source of more confusion than the planner-versus-coordinator debate itself.

Many luxury resorts and private villas in Costa Rica include a "venue coordinator" or "in-house wedding coordinator" in the venue contract. The Four Seasons Papagayo has one. So does Casa Chameleon. So does Andaz, The Springs, Nayara, and most of the high-end villas you have on your shortlist.

**A venue coordinator is not a wedding planner.** Let me say that one more time, clearly, because it is the single most expensive misunderstanding I see couples make.

A venue coordinator works for the venue. Her job is to manage the *venue's* responsibilities — the spaces you are renting, the ceremony chairs the venue provides, the food-and-beverage program the venue's kitchen is cooking, the venue's banquet staff, the venue's setup of the venue's furniture. She is excellent at that work. She is also working on three other weddings that month and is not paid to design yours.

A venue coordinator does not:
- Design your wedding
- Source your florist
- Source your photographer
- Manage your budget
- Coordinate your transport
- Run your timeline across outside vendors
- Handle your welcome dinner if it is at a different property
- Build a rain plan that involves equipment outside the venue's inventory
- Pick up vendors at the airport
- Translate vendor contracts for you
- Tell you that the florist's quote is 30% high for the design they're proposing
- Make sure your photographer is at the right villa at the right time
- Sit with you at the run-through and adjust the ceremony seating because the light is different than expected
- Stay until the last guest is gone

A wedding planner does all of those things.

The reason this misunderstanding is so expensive is that couples often see "wedding coordinator included" on their venue contract and assume they are covered. They are not. They are covered for the *venue's* portion of the wedding, which is real but partial. Everything that happens outside the venue's contracted scope — which is the majority of a luxury wedding — is unmanaged.

If you are working with a venue that includes a coordinator, that is wonderful. Your wedding planner and your venue coordinator will work closely together, and the venue coordinator will be a key partner. But she is not your planner. She is the venue's representative. You still need yours.

### A short decision framework

If you are still unsure which role fits your wedding, walk through these six questions honestly.

**1. Is your wedding in a country where you currently live?**
*No* → You need full-service planning. Don't keep reading. Skip the rest.
*Yes* → Continue.

**2. Is your wedding above 50 guests, or above $50,000 in budget?**
*Yes to either* → You almost certainly need full-service. Continue if you'd like to confirm.
*No to both* → Continue.

**3. Are you planning more than one event (welcome dinner, wedding, brunch)?**
*Yes* → Full-service. The logistics multiply quickly.
*No* → Continue.

**4. Have you already booked your venue, photographer, florist, caterer, and major vendors?**
*Yes* → You may be a candidate for partial planning.
*No* → You need full-service.

**5. Do you have professional experience managing multi-vendor logistics, and do you have the bandwidth in the last 60 days before the wedding to handle final coordination yourself?**
*Yes to both* → Day-of coordination may be enough for a small local wedding. Not for a destination wedding.
*No to either* → Partial or full-service.

**6. Is your wedding small (under 40 guests), local, and at a venue where every vendor is someone you've personally walked the day through with?**
*Yes* → Day-of coordination is appropriate.
*No* → You're back at partial or full-service.

That decision tree routes most couples honestly. If you land at "full-service" and you've been hoping for "day-of," I am not trying to sell you up. I am telling you that the wedding you are imagining is going to cost you in places you cannot see yet without a planner in the loop — and those costs are usually higher than the planning fee you were trying to avoid.

### What "10–12% of budget" actually pays for, in case it sounds abstract

I have been told the planning fee feels like a lot. It is the largest single non-tangible line on a wedding budget. The couple is not eating it, not wearing it, not holding it in their hands, not framing it on the wall.

So here is what is actually inside it.

For a $100,000 wedding with a $10,000 planning fee, that is roughly 300 to 450 hours of work distributed over 14 to 18 months. That is the early discovery calls, the design development, every venue conversation, every vendor sourcing round, every contract review, every WhatsApp message in two languages, every revised budget version, every rain-plan iteration, every walk-through, every rehearsal, the wedding day itself (a 14- to 16-hour day for the lead planner and her team), and the breakdown.

It is also the cost of *not* having to learn any of this yourself. The cost of not waking up at 3am the week of the wedding wondering whether the rental order is right. The cost of being able to walk into the welcome dinner already a guest at your own wedding, not the host.

Couples who have been through the planning process almost always say the same thing afterward, and they say it without prompting: *I cannot believe I considered doing this without a planner.* I include that not as a sales line but because it is the most reliable piece of feedback I have collected over eight years of this work.

### Frequently asked questions

**What's the difference between a wedding planner and a wedding coordinator?**
A wedding planner is engaged 12 to 24 months out and owns the entire wedding — design, vendor sourcing, budget, contracts, logistics, and day-of execution. A wedding coordinator (day-of) is engaged four to eight weeks out and only executes a plan the couple has already built. The planner builds the wedding. The coordinator runs the wedding day.

**Do I need a wedding planner if I have a venue coordinator?**
Yes. A venue coordinator works for the venue and manages the venue's portion of the wedding — the spaces you are renting, the venue's catering and staff, the setup of items the venue provides. She is not designing your wedding, sourcing your outside vendors, or managing your budget. For a luxury or destination wedding, those responsibilities still need an owner, and that owner is your planner.

**Can my venue coordinator replace a wedding planner?**
No. They are different roles with different scopes and different employers. Your venue coordinator works for the property. Your planner works for you. The two work together on the day, and a great venue coordinator is a tremendous partner — but she does not replace a planner any more than the venue's kitchen replaces a wedding cake designer.

**How much does a wedding planner cost in Costa Rica?**
Full-service luxury planning typically runs 10–12% of total budget or a tiered flat fee — roughly $7,500–$10,000 for an intimate wedding, $10,000–$17,500 for a mid-size wedding, and $17,500–$30,000+ for a full-estate or statement wedding. Partial planning is $4,000–$8,000. Day-of coordination is $1,500–$3,500.

**Is a day-of coordinator enough for a destination wedding?**
Almost never. A destination wedding involves vendor relationships, language, regulations, and on-the-ground problem-solving that a coordinator parachuting in four weeks before the wedding cannot supply. The wedding may still happen, but the experience the couple was imagining usually does not.

**What's a "venue coordinator" — and is that the same as a wedding planner?**
A venue coordinator is included in many resort and villa contracts and manages the *venue's* responsibilities on the wedding day: the property's spaces, staff, in-house F&B if applicable, and the setup of items the venue provides. She is not the wedding planner. She does not design the wedding, source outside vendors, manage the budget, or coordinate the dozens of moving parts outside her venue's scope. Couples who assume their venue coordinator replaces a planner consistently end up doing the planner's work themselves, in a country they don't live in, often in a language they don't speak.

**When should I hire a wedding planner for a Costa Rica wedding?**
For full-service: 12 to 18 months out for a mid-size wedding, 18 to 24 months out for a full-estate or statement wedding. The top private villas — The Point, Vista Hermosa Estate, Casa Chameleon, Villa Punto de Vista, Castle of Oz, Rancho Pacifico — book dry-season Saturdays 14 to 18 months in advance. Hiring a planner before you book the venue is the move I recommend most often.

**Can I skip a planner if I'm having an elopement?**
For a true two-person elopement, sometimes — though even then, a planner or elopement specialist handles the officiant, the permits, the photography, and the legal paperwork. For a small ceremony of 6 to 20 guests, which is what most "elopements" actually are now, the answer is closer to "you still want a planner, just a scaled-down engagement." A small wedding is not the same thing as a simple wedding.

**Is a partial planner enough for a destination wedding?**
Sometimes — but only if the couple has done extensive work on the front end with vendors and a venue they know well. The risk with partial planning at a destination is that the planner inherits decisions made without full local knowledge, and some of those decisions may already be costing the couple money or quality. If you are leaning partial, build in an early consult with the planner *before* you book the venue and major vendors.

**What's the difference between a destination wedding planner and a local wedding planner?**
A destination wedding planner is based in the destination country (here, Costa Rica) and produces weddings there year-round. She is in the vendor relationships, knows the regional weather, speaks the local language, and has done this hundreds of times. A "destination wedding planner" based in your home country who flies in for the wedding is a different model — sometimes good, often more expensive, almost always reliant on a local Costa Rica team underneath the surface anyway.

**Do you have to use the planner the venue recommends?**
No, but the venue's preferred-vendor list is usually a real signal of quality. The planners on those lists have produced weddings at the property before, know the staff, know the layout, and have a working relationship with the venue coordinator. If you have a planner you love who isn't on the list, that's fine — but bring her into the venue conversation early so the venue isn't a surprise.

**Can I just hire my photographer's coordinator friend instead of a real planner?**
This is a question I get often, and the honest answer is: photographers sometimes have a coordinator they recommend, and sometimes that coordinator is excellent for a simple local wedding. But the photographer's recommendation is rarely a vetted full-service planner, and the work scopes are different. Hire the right role for the wedding, not the most convenient one.

**What if I've already booked everything and just need help executing the day?**
Then you are a candidate for partial or day-of planning, depending on how complete the planning actually is. The first call will tell us which one. Bring your venue contract, your major vendor contracts, your budget, and your timeline. We can usually figure out which role fits within forty-five minutes.

**How early should I have the planner conversation?**
Before you book the venue, if at all possible. The venue decision is the single most consequential decision in a Costa Rica wedding, and it is the one couples most often make based on Instagram aesthetic before they understand the cost and logistics implications. A thirty-minute call with a planner before you sign the venue contract can save you 12 months of friction.

**Is a wedding planner worth it for a small wedding?**
Yes, for a destination wedding, almost always. The work is not proportional to the guest count — many of the logistics, the design hours, the vendor coordination, and the regulatory work happen the same way at 30 guests as at 90. A small destination wedding is not a small project. The planning fee scales down for a small wedding, but the value does not.

### What I would tell you if we were sitting across from each other right now

I would tell you that the question is not "planner or coordinator." The question is "what does this wedding actually need to land well, and who do you trust to land it."

If you are reading this and you have already started planning your Costa Rica wedding without a planner, you have not made a mistake — you are exactly where many of my favorite couples were when they first wrote to me. Bring me what you have. We will figure out the right fit. Sometimes it is full-service. Sometimes it is partial. Sometimes I will tell you, honestly, that you can do this yourself and that a great day-of coordinator three weeks out is all you need. I would rather tell you that than take on a wedding you did not need me for.

But if you are imagining a multi-day private-villa wedding, in a country you do not live in, in a language you do not speak fluently, in a vendor ecosystem you do not know — and you are wondering whether a four-week day-of coordinator can carry it for you — I am telling you, plainly, no. That is not where day-of works. That is where planners exist.

This work is precise. The role you hire is the entry.
